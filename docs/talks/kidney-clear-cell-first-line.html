Kidney cancer that has spread: choosing the first treatment

1

What we found

The cancer started in the kidney and has spread to other parts of the body. It is the clear cell type, the most common kind of kidney cancer.

Treatment usually can't cure it, but today's medicines can keep it under control for years, and for some people much longer. The goal is to help you live longer and feel well.

2

How these medicines work

Immunotherapy (IO) takes the brakes off your immune system so it can attack the cancer. Nivolumab (Opdivo) and pembrolizumab (Keytruda) release one brake, PD-1. Ipilimumab (Yervoy) releases a second brake, CTLA-4.

Targeted pills (TKIs) cut off the blood supply the cancer needs to grow: axitinib (Inlyta), cabozantinib (Cabometyx) and lenvatinib (Lenvima).

Two immunotherapy drugs together is called IO + IO. One immunotherapy drug plus a targeted pill is called IO + TKI.

Your choices

Tap a choice to see it in the picture below and in the table of schedules and side effects.

Both approaches help people live longer than the older pill, sunitinib, and both can shrink tumors a lot. They differ in how fast and how often they shrink the cancer, and in how long the benefit lasts.

For a few people whose cancer is small and growing slowly, careful watching with regular scans before starting treatment is also an option. Surgery or radiation to a single spot can sometimes help too.

How the two differ

1. Shrinking the tumor quickly

IO + TKI shrinks tumors in more people, and fewer see their cancer grow at the first scans. This can matter if the cancer is causing symptoms or growing quickly.

2. Long-lasting, deep responses

IO + IO shrinks tumors in fewer people, but when it works the benefit often lasts a very long time, sometimes many years. A small but real group stay well controlled long after treatment stops. How long the shrinking lasted, for half the people whose tumors shrank:

Schedule and side effects

Numbers are people out of 100, at any level unless marked severe. There are no placebo studies here, so the older pill, sunitinib, is the comparison. IO + IO comes from CheckMate 214 and counts side effects judged to be from treatment. IO + TKI is the range across the three studies, some of which counted every side effect whatever the cause, so its numbers may read a little high. Immune side effects can affect any organ and can start months after the last dose. Most are treatable, often with steroids. Tell us early about any side effect: holding a dose or lowering the pill dose usually helps.

Also part of your care

Surgery or radiation

If the kidney with the tumor is still in place, removing it can help some people. A few spots can sometimes be treated with surgery or focused radiation.

Blood tests and scans

Regular blood tests check the thyroid, liver, kidneys and blood sugar for immune side effects. Scans every 2 to 3 months show how the treatment is working.

Blood pressure and your other doctors

Targeted pills raise blood pressure, so check it at home. Tell every doctor you are on immunotherapy, because its side effects can look like other illnesses.

If the cancer grows later

Other good options remain, including different targeted pills, belzutifan (Welireg) and clinical trials.

Also

Call us right away if you have

  • Diarrhea 4 or more times a day above your usual, or blood or mucus in your stool
  • A new cough, shortness of breath or chest pain (call 911 if severe)
  • Yellow skin or eyes, dark urine, or pain on the right side of your belly
  • Severe tiredness, headaches, dizziness, or feeling very thirsty
  • A fever of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher
  • Blood pressure above the level your team gave you, or a severe headache
  • A rash with blisters, or sores in your mouth that stop you eating or drinking

Immune side effects can start even months after the last dose. Tell any doctor or emergency room that you have had immunotherapy.
