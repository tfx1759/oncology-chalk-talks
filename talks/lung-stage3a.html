Stage III lung cancer: radiation or surgery?

1

What we found

The cancer started in the lung. It is stage IIIA: it has reached lymph nodes in the chest or grown large, but scans show no spread to other parts of the body.

That matters: the goal of treatment is cure. Every approach combines several treatments, and immunotherapy now adds to the chance of cure.

Immunotherapy works poorly, and can add side effects, when the cancer is driven by certain gene changes such as EGFR or ALK. Those cancers are treated with targeted pills instead. Whether surgery is possible is decided by a lung surgeon, often with a team review (tumor board), using scans, lymph node biopsies and breathing tests.

2

How the two approaches work

Immunotherapy takes a brake (PD-1 or PD-L1) off your immune system so it can find and attack cancer cells. Durvalumab (Imfinzi), pembrolizumab (Keytruda) and nivolumab (Opdivo) all work this way.

In option B, giving treatment before surgery shrinks the cancer and lets us see how well it worked: in about 1 in 5 people, no living cancer is left when the surgeon removes it.

Your choices

Tap a choice to see it in the picture below and in the table of schedules and side effects.

Both approaches aim for cure, and in each, adding immunotherapy helped more people live longer than the older treatment. They have never been compared with each other in a study.

If a gene change such as EGFR or ALK is found, immunotherapy is usually left out. A targeted pill is added instead: osimertinib (Tagrisso) after chemoradiation or after surgery for EGFR, or alectinib (Alecensa) after surgery for ALK. If neither approach is safe, chemo and radiation can be given one after the other, or radiation alone.

Reading this picture
  • Do not compare option A with option B directly. The studies enrolled different people. Option B studies included smaller, earlier cancers (stage II) and only people a surgeon judged could have surgery. Option A counted only people who had already finished chemoradiation without the cancer growing.
  • Option B blends two studies, CheckMate 816 and KEYNOTE-671. AEGEAN, a third study, gave similar early results but has not yet reported 5-year survival.
  • What the studies show is that adding immunotherapy helps in both approaches. Which approach suits you depends mostly on your surgeon's view and your health.
For your doctor
PACIFIC (unresectable stage III, PS 0–1, no progression after ≥2 cycles platinum concurrent with 54–66 Gy; durvalumab 10 mg/kg q2w or placebo up to 12 months, started 1–42 days after RT), 5-year update (Spigel, JCO 2022): OS 42.9 vs 33.4%, HR 0.72 (0.59–0.89), median 47.5 vs 29.1 mo; PFS 33.1 vs 19.0%, HR 0.55. Option B blends the two trials with 5-year data. CheckMate 816 (stage IB–IIIA AJCC 7, 3 cycles nivolumab + chemo vs chemo, no adjuvant IO; known EGFR/ALK excluded): pCR 24.0 vs 2.2%; surgery 83.2 vs 75.4%; final OS (Forde, NEJM 2025) 5-year 65.4 vs 55.0%, HR 0.72 (0.52–1.00); 5-year EFS 49.2 vs 34.4%, HR 0.68; reported OS benefit mainly in PD-L1 ≥1%. KEYNOTE-671 (stage II–IIIB N2, 4 cycles pembrolizumab or placebo + cisplatin doublet, then 13 cycles adjuvant; EGFR/ALK not excluded, about 3–4% each): pCR 18.1 vs 4.0%; surgery 82.1 vs 79.4%; 36-month OS 71 vs 64%, HR 0.72 (Spicer, Lancet 2024); 5-year OS 64.6 vs 53.6%, HR 0.74, EFS 49.9 vs 26.5% (ESMO 2025 LBA, taken from meeting reports). AEGEAN (stage IIA–IIIB, 4 cycles durvalumab or placebo + chemo, then 12 cycles adjuvant; EGFR/ALK excluded): pCR 17.2 vs 4.3%; 24-month EFS 63.3 vs 52.4%, HR 0.68 (Heymach, NEJM 2023); OS immature. Blends are simple means: OS 65 vs 54, EFS 50 vs 30, pCR 20 (all three trials) vs 4, reached surgery 82 vs 78. CheckMate 77T (perioperative nivolumab) is a fourth option of the same kind. EGFR: PACIFIC EGFR subgroup showed no clear benefit (n=35; PFS HR 0.91, OS HR 1.02); LAURA osimertinib after CRT, PFS HR 0.16 (Lu, NEJM 2024). ADAURA and ALINA support adjuvant osimertinib and alectinib. NCCN advises at least EGFR, ALK and PD-L1 testing before choosing perioperative therapy for resectable IB–IIIB. Note: EMA restricts durvalumab after CRT to PD-L1 ≥1%; the FDA label does not.

Schedule and side effects

Rows marked “added by immunotherapy” compare immunotherapy with a placebo or with the same treatment without immunotherapy, so they show the extra risk on top of what chemo, radiation or surgery already cause. Option A comes from PACIFIC (durvalumab vs placebo after chemoradiation). Option B comes from KEYNOTE-671 and AEGEAN (immunotherapy vs placebo, both with the same chemo and surgery) and CheckMate 816 (vs chemo alone). Immune side effects can affect any organ and can start months after the last dose. Most are treatable, often with steroids. Tell us early about any side effect.

Also part of your care

Tests before we start

A PET scan and brain MRI make sure the cancer has not spread. A biopsy of the chest lymph nodes (often by bronchoscopy) confirms the stage. Gene tests on the tumor (EGFR, ALK and others) and PD-L1 come back in 1 to 3 weeks.

A team decision

A lung surgeon, a radiation doctor and your oncologist review your case together. Breathing tests and a heart check show whether surgery is safe.

Stopping smoking

If you smoke, stopping now lowers the risk of problems after surgery and radiation and helps treatment work. We can help with medicines and counseling.

After treatment

CT scans every few months for the first years watch for the cancer coming back. Staying active and breathing exercises help recovery.

Also

Call us right away if you have

  • A fever of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher
  • A new or worse cough, shortness of breath or chest pain (call 911 if severe)
  • Pain on swallowing that stops you eating or drinking
  • Diarrhea 4 or more times a day above your usual, or blood in your stool
  • Coughing up blood
  • Yellow skin or eyes, or dark urine
  • Severe tiredness, headaches or dizziness

Immune side effects can start even months after the last dose. Tell any doctor or emergency room that you have had immunotherapy.
