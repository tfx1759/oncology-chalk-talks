DCIS: should I take a hormone pill after surgery?

1

What we found

DCIS means abnormal cells were growing inside the milk ducts. They had not broken through the duct wall. That is why it is called stage 0.

While it stays inside the duct, DCIS can't spread to other parts of the body. Surgery removed it.

The question now is how much to lower the chance of a new breast cancer later.

2

What could happen later

In the same breast

DCIS can come back where it started. Roughly half the time it comes back as DCIS again, and half the time as invasive cancer.

Radiation after lumpectomy lowers this chance by about half. The MSK calculator above estimates it for you.

In the other breast

A new, separate cancer can start in the other breast. Over 10 years this happens to about 4 to 8 in 100 women after DCIS.

A hormone pill lowers the chance of both, most clearly in the other breast. It does not change survival, which is already excellent: about 97 in 100 women treated for DCIS do not die of breast cancer in the next 20 years, with or without a pill.

So this choice is about lowering the chance of going through breast cancer treatment again, weighed against side effects.

Side effects to weigh

“More in 100” is the added risk compared with women taking a placebo (a dummy pill) in large trials: NSABP B-24 and P-1 for tamoxifen, TAM-01 for low-dose tamoxifen, and IBIS-II for anastrozole. Many of these, like hot flashes and aches, are common on a placebo too. Most side effects ease after the first few months. If one pill doesn't suit you, switching often helps, so tell us rather than stopping.
